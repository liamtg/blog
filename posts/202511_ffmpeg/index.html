We All Need to Speak AI

It’s trite to say that large language models will fundamentally reshape the way we work. What’s less clear is how that reshaping will actually happen in practice.

I frequently find myself doing tasks I really am not qualified to be doing. Recently a case in point was creating a demo video for a new product feature. Unfortunately, I was not blessed with a high degree of artistic ability (my art teacher once described my drawings as “unique,” which was not a compliment).

So normally I would outsource any kind of video work. But since in this case the prototype lived on my machine, and we were also under pretty significant time pressure, I decided I would pull this together myself.

It was an elucidating journey. What started as a mildly desperate attempt to produce a video before a deadline ended up giving me some new insight into some ways that AI may fundamentally reconfigure the nature of the work we do, and, more practically, the tools that make sense in an AI-native workflow.

GUI doesn’t work with AI

To start with, generative AI video tools were a non-starter. They are amazing, and even though I love playing with them with my kids, I have yet to find a convincing use for them in a business context (other than, say, fun social media content). I have no doubt they would be useful in the hands of a skilled, artistic creator who wanted to take their already solid foundation to another level. But I needed something to do the basics - I didn’t want something snazzy or slick . I simply wanted to transform my raw footage into a useful demonstration in a way that would highlight and convey the key information to potential clients.

I also quickly rejected using AI with a video editor, at least as my primary tool. The problem is that video-editing software is built for humans - it’s built around clicking, dragging, nudging boxes pixel by pixel, and relying on the magical instincts of people who actually know what they’re doing. AI in its current form cannot use these tools the way a human does. The best “AI-augmented” workflow I could come up with was to clearly describe each objective to the AI, and ask them to write a set of instructions to achieve what I wanted in my video editor of choice. In fairness, this was pretty useful for someone (like me) who was little direct experience with these tools, but still nowhere near the game changer I was looking for.

I then decided to try something different - use a command line tool. A command line tool is a tool that you interact with by writing your instructions in your terminal (command line). This is where things became interesting, because all of a sudden you are in the realm of working with commands and scripts. And this is, of course, something that AI is very, very good at.

The tool I went with was FFmpeg. FFmpeg is a powerful command-line tool for working with audio and video: you can use it to convert between formats, trim and join clips, extract audio, resize or re-encode video, add subtitles, and a lot more. Many media editing apps use it under the hood.

Two years ago, FFmpeg would make little sense for a use case like this, certainly for me. It’s low-level, fiddly, and demands not only learning the tool but also writing a significant amount of scripting to get anything useful out of it. Even if you do invest the time to understand it, you still have to build and maintain the scripts yourself. For a one-off video, or even for a series of videos, the return on that investment just isn’t there.

But in the days where LLMs can write code, how much does the equation change?

The power of scripting with AI

For this project, my main interface to FFmpeg was Claude Code, an AI coding assistant. For some of the more challenging aspects, I supplemented this with Open AI’s Codex coding agent.

I had already designed the video - I had planned out the segments and how they would look. I had largely decided how I wanted to combine different video footage, along with the graphics, music, captions and overlays. I already had all the raw footage of the recordings. So the task became: how to “transform” the raw footage into the final video.

The first key learning was the importance of locking everything down into scripts. My initial approach was to describe what I wanted, let the LLM use FFmpeg to generate a solution, tweak it a few times, and move on. In hindsight it should have been obvious that this was a dead end: it was hard to tweak later, impossible to repeat reliably, and nearly impossible to keep consistent across the project.

However, once I committed to capturing each step in a script, everything started to fall into place. It became clear that the most effective way to approach the project was to treat the video as something assembled from smaller, well-defined operations. My first approach of writing a separate script for each scene was quickly abandoned. I quickly realised that the better approach was to work with the LLM to create a small collection of reusable primitives: overlays, transitions, speed adjustments and the like. Each of these lived as its own script, simple enough to understand at a glance and easy to refine when needed.

Once those building blocks existed, I could combine them into an orchestrated pipeline. The pipeline took the raw footage, applied the relevant operations and assembled the segments into a complete video.

This process then become entirely deterministic and repeatable, and this was the most valuable outcome. If a piece of footage needed to be re-recorded, I could replace the file and run the pipeline again. The entire video rebuilt itself consistently, without any of the manual nudging and re-aligning that normally comes with video editing. If I needed to produce a similar demo for a different feature, I could reuse the same structure and adjust the inputs.

The hard work went into defining the components and making sure they behaved predictably. After that, producing a new version of the video became largely mechanical. The combination of explicit tools, and an LLM capable of generating and adjusting them, changed the whole return-on-investment calculation of using a low-level command line tool. A tool I would once have avoided became both approachable and efficient.

The value of a repeatable pipeline

The real breakthrough wasn’t the final video, but the repeatable process that emerged. When working with screen recordings, I almost always spot numerous sections I want to improve or modify, requiring either I re-record the video and re-configure everything again, or simply live with whatever problem I had identified.

Previously, any changes to the raw footage required tedious rounds of mechanical re-editing to incorporate the new footage and make everything line up again. But with the FFmpeg pipeline, I simply re-ran the compilation again, and perhaps make some minor tweaks to the settings, for example if some timings had changed. Everything rebuilt itself cleanly. This fundamentally changed the cost of correcting mistakes: a decision that previously meant an hour of fiddling now took minutes to correct.

In the end, the time I spent experimenting and creating this first video using this new paradigm was similar to what I probably would have spent inside a traditional editor. But for me, that investment was hands down worth it. The result looked far more professional than anything I’ve managed with conventional tools. And of course, I now have a highly efficient and customisable video editing harness that can be used on my future demo videos.

Returning to Command Line Tools

Importantly, the whole process felt completely different to my previous video creation attempts. Instead of wrestling with a canvas, nudging objects around and guessing whether everything lined up, the agent handled the presentation work mathematically. Margins, alignment, transitions and timings all became precise operations expressed in code. That suits how I operate far more than trying to manipulate a GUI, and it meant the final output wasn’t constrained by my lack of artistic instinct (or patience).

Of course, part of this is simply that I am not a video editor. I don’t have the skill set or the familiarity with those tools. But that’s exactly what made this approach so effective. Using the command-line tool in combination with an AI assistant meant that I was in a sense working with a skilled coding colleague, who could handle technical change and do the grunt work, while still leaving me in full control of the workflow. It opened up a level of capability that is an order of magnitude beyond what I would otherwise have had.

Before this project, FFmpeg belonged firmly in the category of “impressive but impractical.” With an LLM handling the boilerplate and syntax, it became something completely different. The low-level detail stopped being a barrier and instead became a strength. I could ask for a specific operation, read the script, refine it, and then reuse it confidently.

All of this led to a broader realisation: the old, slightly unfriendly command-line tools make far more sense in the AI era than the polished graphical ones built for human intuition. They are the perfect partner for humans working side by side with AI. Every detail of what they’re doing is exposed to the LLM. Nothing is hidden behind menus or icons. LLMs may struggle to use graphical editors in the way a human would, but they can reason about explicit, text-based primitives extremely well.

In trying to solve a practical problem I have little aptitude for, I ended up stumbling into a much bigger realisation: in the era of AI, we will need to rethink all the assumptions we have made about our tooling. And once you see that shift, it becomes difficult to look at modern software, or at your own workflows, in the same way.

If we want to work effectively in an AI-native world, we’re going to have to learn how to speak that language.
